# Track 1 demands visible handoffs, not clinical accuracy

Track 1 of the Austin Agentic AI Hackathon (AWS-hosted, October 23, 2026, sponsored by Arpio and Posit) asks for a coordinated set of five healthcare personas (Lab Analyst, Radiologist, Doctor, Nutritionist, Physio) that ingest lab results, radiology images and EKG time-series from synthetic or public data and turn them into patient-friendly diagnoses, prescriptions, nutrition plans and exercise guidance; the only hard demo requirements are **one patient scenario, at least two visible specialist handoffs, and a clear patient-facing result**, with synthetic data only and an explicit "not medical advice" boundary ([Track 1](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-1.html)). No judging rubric, submission format, deadline or team rules are published anywhere on the site, so the five-item "HCLS guardrails" list (de-identified data, explicit failure handling, auditable evidence trail, red-flag escalation with human confirmation, encryption) is the closest thing to a scoring sheet. Every team gets the same baseline: a temporary Workshop Studio account under `WSParticipantRole`, a browser VS Code or Kiro desktop, and a 4.7 KB starter kit that smoke-tests Bedrock Converse and deploys one AgentCore Runtime, defaulting to the `us.openai.gpt-5.6-luna` inference profile ([hackathon-kit.zip](https://atx.rahman.dev/hackathon-kit.zip)). Two of the page's four dataset links are effectively unusable in a one-day window (MIMIC-CXR-JPG needs multi-day PhysioNet credentialing and the PathVQA Hugging Face link returns 401), and the page lists no EKG dataset at all, so the practical data stack is **PTB-XL for ECG, the 100-patient MIMIC-IV demo for labs, Open-i for chest X-rays with real reports, and Synthea Coherent for a linked synthetic patient**. On the build side, the AWS-recommended path is Strands agents-as-tools on AgentCore Runtime; Bedrock Agents Classic has been closed to new customers since July 30, 2026 and is not an option. The evidence from comparable projects is unambiguous about what wins: a separate critic/safety agent, per-claim grounding shown on screen, deterministic escalation rules, a human sign-off field, and a tiny eval table beat extra personas and accuracy claims every time.

## The brief rewards five perspectives becoming one understandable answer

The track page frames the problem as "make complex signals feel human": healthcare guidance "often depends on connecting structured results, images, time-series data, and everyday behavior," and the agents "should show how specialists can collaborate while keeping the final experience understandable" ([Track 1](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-1.html)). The suggested four-step agent flow is ingest (labs, radiology images, EKG time-series from synthetic/public sources), route (each input to the persona best suited to analyze it), combine (into diagnoses, prescriptions, nutrition and exercise plans) and expose (data provenance, uncertainty and reasoning behind the final response). The build brief's one sentence of tactical advice is worth internalizing: "Make the handoffs visible. A strong prototype can show which persona reviewed each input, what it found, and how the combined result becomes patient-friendly guidance." The closing "good demo question" restates the whole track: "Can your team show how five specialized perspectives become one answer that a patient could actually understand?"

The five personas are named consistently in three places, but the demo checklist requires only **two** handoffs, so a minimum viable demo can run three agents (for example Lab Analyst to Doctor to Nutritionist) and still satisfy the brief. The three input modalities map naturally onto Lab Analyst (structured), Radiologist (images) and, implicitly, Doctor (EKG is never assigned to a persona on the page), while Nutritionist and Physio are output-side personas that produce plans rather than read data. That asymmetry is a design hint: the output personas are the cheapest to build (pure LLM reasoning over the Doctor's summary) and the ones that make the final result feel patient-centered.

The HCLS guardrails list functions as the de facto rubric. Verbatim, it asks teams to use synthetic or public data only and never send PHI to unapproved endpoints; to "handle failures explicitly" so that a failed image or lab step requests clinical re-evaluation instead of crashing; to "record the evidence, agent handoffs, tool calls, uncertainty, and final outputs so the result can be audited"; to "flag red-flag values, such as severe hypoxia, and require human confirmation before any care-related action"; and to use approved AWS boundaries with encryption at rest and in transit ([Track 1](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-1.html)). Each of those five lines maps onto a screen element a judge can see: a synthetic-data banner, a graceful-failure path, an audit log panel, a red-flag interrupt with a confirm button, and S3/KMS defaults.

What is missing matters as much. Neither the landing page nor any track page publishes judging criteria, scoring weights, a submission mechanism (repo, video or form), deadlines, team-size rules or prizes; registration runs through an AWS Quick form that contains no rules text ([Landing page](https://atx.rahman.dev/)). The landing page names six AWS judges whose backgrounds span solutions architecture, analytics and AI, enterprise support and technical account management (Muni Chada, Emilio Herrera, Kalyan Kumar Neelampudi, Whitney Waldinger, Abhishek Maligehalli Shivalingaiah, Mark Searcy), which is a panel of AWS practitioners rather than clinicians: expect them to weigh fluency with Bedrock/AgentCore and operational hygiene at least as heavily as clinical plausibility. Track 2's "Make it credible" panel, which asks for "a simple timeline, threshold, or comparison so judges can see why the system raised a signal," and Track 3's "specialist analyst agents feed a portfolio-manager agent that reconciles them" flow confirm the house pattern across all three tracks: specialists feed a coordinator, provenance and uncertainty are explicit, a human stays in the loop, and the demo is three beats long ([Track 2](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-2.html); [Track 3](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-3.html)).

## Joining takes three screens and the starter kit takes four commands

Onboarding is identical on every track page. Open a fresh or private browser window, sign in to Workshop Studio (email one-time password, Login with Amazon, or Amazon employee SSO), enter the 12-digit event access code from the event operator (the page pre-fills `a52a-010f17-28` in its join link), accept the terms, and open the temporary AWS account; then confirm in the console that you are `WSParticipantRole` in the workshop region ([Workshop Studio join](https://catalog.us-east-1.prod.workshops.aws/join?access-code=a52a-010f17-28); [Track 1](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-1.html)). A 159 MB login walkthrough video is linked for anyone who gets stuck. The catalog host, the kit README and the Positron script all default to `us-east-1`, so treat that as the workshop region unless the operator says otherwise. Workshop Studio can also hand out temporary CLI credentials, but they **expire after 60 minutes**, so anyone working from a local terminal should plan to re-copy them.

After joining, the Event dashboard's `Event outputs` section exposes two IDEs. `VScodeIdeURL` (recommended) opens a browser VS Code on the shared `/workshop` folder with an integrated terminal; `KiroRDPConnectionURL` opens Kiro on an Ubuntu desktop over one-click RDP, with fallback credentials in `KiroRDPCredentials` that the page explicitly says not to paste into notes or chat ([Workshop Studio dashboard](https://catalog.us-east-1.prod.workshops.aws/event/dashboard/en-US)). The page's own guidance is that VS Code is "the fastest web-based path" and Kiro is for teams that want a full Linux desktop. An optional third path bootstraps Positron (Posit's data-science IDE) on SageMaker Studio via `curl -fsSL https://atx.rahman.dev/setup_positron_sagemaker_from_private_ecr.py -o positron.py && uv run positron.py`; the script creates a SageMaker domain, execution role, user profile, Space and JupyterLab app on `ml.t3.xlarge` with a custom Positron image from a private ECR, attaches AmazonBedrockFullAccess and BedrockAgentCoreFullAccess, and the page warns setup "can take 20-30 minutes" ([Positron script](https://atx.rahman.dev/setup_positron_sagemaker_from_private_ecr.py)). On a one-day clock that is a cost worth paying only if the notebook workflow is central to your idea, for example if someone is going to run an ECG classifier or plot time-series inline.

The starter kit is the same on all three tracks and is deliberately tiny: `README.md`, `agent.py`, `infer.py`, `setup_agentcore_role.py`, `requirements.txt` (`boto3`, `bedrock-agentcore`, `bedrock-agentcore-starter-toolkit`). The README's four steps are the fastest way to a working baseline ([hackathon-kit.zip](https://atx.rahman.dev/hackathon-kit.zip)):

| Step | What it does | Commands / expected result |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Check environment | Confirms region, identity, Python | `export AWS_REGION=us-east-1`, `aws sts get-caller-identity`, `python3 --version` |
| 2. Direct inference | Bedrock Converse smoke test with the workshop role | `python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt && .venv/bin/python infer.py` returns `OK` |
| 3. AgentCore role | Creates `hackathon-gpt-agentcore-execution-role` trusted by `bedrock-agentcore.amazonaws.com` with an inline policy allowing `bedrock:InvokeModel*` on the inference profile and its routed models | needs `iam:CreateRole`, `iam:PutRolePolicy`, `iam:PassRole`; set a unique `AGENTCORE_ROLE_NAME` if sharing an account |
| 4. Deploy runtime | `agentcore configure --entrypoint agent.py --name workshop_gpt_agent --deployment-type direct_code_deploy ... --disable-otel --disable-memory`, then `agentcore deploy` and `agentcore invoke '{"prompt":"Reply with OK."}'` | returns `{"model":"us.openai.gpt-5.6-luna","text":"OK"}` |

`agent.py` is a 20-line `BedrockAgentCoreApp` whose `@app.entrypoint` handler takes `{"prompt": ...}`, calls `bedrock-runtime.converse` with `maxTokens=128, temperature=0`, and returns `{"model","text"}`; the README says to "replace the handler logic with your hackathon project after this baseline works." Three constraints in that README deserve a highlight. First, **model access runs through a workshop allowlist of Bedrock inference profiles**: the default is GPT-5.6 Luna (`us.openai.gpt-5.6-luna`) and the tested alternatives are GPT-5.4, GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Terra, all `us.openai.*` profiles; the README tells you to verify with `aws bedrock list-inference-profiles --region "$AWS_REGION"`, warns that base `openai.gpt-5.6-*` IDs "may not support on-demand throughput," and notes that "a Bedrock API key does not override an IAM explicit deny." Whether Claude or Nova models are also enabled in the participant accounts is not stated anywhere, which matters because every linked aws-samples repo was written against Claude Sonnet; run the list-profiles command in the first ten minutes and pin your model choice to what is actually allowlisted. Second, the IAM surface is scoped: role creation, AgentCore runtime creation/invocation and the S3 code bucket for direct code deploy all require permissions the participant role "may or may not grant," and "the script cannot grant permissions to itself." Third, the direct-inference call and the AgentCore invocation use **different roles**, so the troubleshooting table's "direct inference works, AgentCore inference fails" row is the most likely failure you will hit; the fix is to check the execution role's inline policy against the exact profile ARN. The README closes with a cleanup reminder, since each active runtime incurs charges; at hackathon scale AgentCore Runtime pricing ($0.0895/vCPU-hr and $0.00945/GB-hr for microVM v1, billed per second) amounts to cents, but cleanup is part of the "approved AWS boundaries" story judges expect ([AgentCore pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/agentcore/pricing/)).

One tooling caveat the kit does not mention: the Strands deployment docs now recommend the newer npm CLI (`npm install -g @aws/agentcore`, `agentcore create / dev / deploy / invoke`) and explicitly say to uninstall the older `bedrock-agentcore-starter-toolkit` to avoid conflicts ([Strands docs: Deploy to AgentCore](https://strandsagents.com/latest/documentation/docs/user-guide/deploy/deploy_to_bedrock_agentcore/python/)). The kit uses the older toolkit. Pick one and do not mix them in the same environment; the kit's path is the one the organizers tested in these accounts, so stay on it unless it breaks.

## Only three of the page's six data links are usable today

The "Resources to explore" section lists four datasets and six "sample repositories" and nothing else (no AWS documentation links, no PDFs), under the instruction to "start with de-identified or synthetic data, then use the sample repositories as references for multimodal analysis, healthcare workflows, and Bedrock-based agents" ([Track 1](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-1.html)). The datasets, with their real-world status, are:

| Page listing | What it actually is | One-day usability |
|---|---|---|
| MultiCaRe (Kaggle) | ~96,000 de-identified PubMed Central case reports with ~135,000 images, clinical histories, blood work and radiology; CC BY-NC-SA with per-item licenses; Zenodo v2.1 and a Hugging Face mirror exist ([PMC10792687](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10792687); [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/14994046); [HF mirror](https://huggingface.co/datasets/OpenMed/multicare-images/blob/main/README.md)) | Usable with a free Kaggle account; the richest single source of *narrative* cases with labs and images, ideal for scripting the "one patient scenario" |
| PathVQA and MedVQA (`flant5/pathvqa` on HF) | PathVQA is 4,998 pathology images with 32,799 QA pairs ([arXiv 2003.10286](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10286v1)); the linked HF repo returned HTTP 401, and the blurb's "CT, X-ray" describes MedVQA generally, not PathVQA | Use the official UCSD-AI4H GitHub release instead; pathology-only, so marginal for the five personas |
| NIH ChestX-ray14 (Kaggle) | 112,120 frontal chest X-rays from 30,805 patients with 14 NLP-mined labels; "no restrictions on the use" with attribution; no free-text reports ([HF mirror](https://huggingface.co/datasets/chehablab/NIHChestXR); [Google Cloud terms page](https://docs.cloud.google.com/healthcare-api/docs/resources/public-datasets/nih-chest)) | Usable, but ~42 GB; download a few hundred PNGs plus the CSV, not the archive |
| MIMIC-CXR-JPG (links to physionet.org home) | 377,110 JPGs with reports-derived labels; requires a credentialed PhysioNet account, CITI training and a signed DUA, per person ([PhysioNet](https://physionet.org/content/mimic-cxr-jpg/2.1.0/)) | Not usable in one day; the page itself says "review PhysioNet access requirements" |

The page lists no EKG dataset even though EKG time-series is one of the three required input modalities, so the gap has to be filled from outside. **PTB-XL** is the best single choice: 21,799 ten-second 12-lead ECGs from 18,869 patients, CC BY 4.0, 1.7 GB, WFDB format readable with the Python `wfdb` package, with a `ptbxl_database.csv` that carries SCP diagnostic codes, a free-text cardiologist `report` string and stratified folds, and it lives on a public S3 bucket you can sync with `aws s3 sync --no-sign-request s3://physionet-open/ptb-xl/1.0.3/ .` ([PhysioNet PTB-XL](https://physionet.org/content/ptb-xl/1.0.3/)). The free-text report is the detail that makes it hackathon-friendly: a "Cardiologist" or Doctor persona can reason over the report and the numeric intervals rather than the raw waveform. MIT-BIH Arrhythmia (48 half-hour records, 73.5 MB, ODC-By, same public bucket) is the fallback if you want rhythm strips with beat annotations ([PhysioNet MIT-BIH](https://physionet.org/content/mitdb/1.0.0/)), and the Chapman-Shaoxing/Ningbo set adds 45,152 ECGs under CC BY 4.0 if volume matters ([PhysioNet ecg-arrhythmia](https://physionet.org/content/ecg-arrhythmia/1.0.0/)). MIMIC-IV-ECG's waveforms are open under ODbL and the AWS registry still advertises an S3 copy, but the registry prefix listed empty when checked and the clinical linkage requires credentialed MIMIC-IV, so treat it as HTTPS-only and unlinked ([AWS registry MIMIC-IV-ECG](https://registry.opendata.aws/mimic-iv-ecg/)).

For labs, the **MIMIC-IV Clinical Database Demo** (100 patients, 15.4 MB, ODbL, all 26 tables including `labevents` and `d_labitems` with reference ranges, no notes) loads into SQLite or DuckDB in seconds and is explicitly intended "for workshops" ([PhysioNet MIMIC-IV demo](https://physionet.org/content/mimic-iv-demo/2.2/)); the eICU demo (>2,500 ICU stays from 20 hospitals, 130 MB, with `lab`, vitals, medication and diagnosis tables plus a SQLite file) is the multi-center alternative ([PhysioNet eICU demo](https://physionet.org/content/eicu-crd-demo/2.0.1/)). For FHIR-native work, Synthea's pre-generated R4 bundles (~95 MB zips) contain `Observation` resources with LOINC-coded CBC, CMP, lipid and HbA1c values ([synthea-sample-data](https://github.com/synthetichealth/synthea-sample-data)), and HealthLake can preload Synthea at datastore creation, the only preload type it supports ([CloudFormation reference](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSCloudFormation/latest/TemplateReference/aws-properties-healthlake-fhirdatastore-preloaddataconfig.html)).

For radiology with real reports, **Open-i / Indiana University** is the only credential-free set that pairs chest X-rays with full findings/impression text (roughly 7,470 images and 3,955 reports, CC BY-NC-ND on the Kaggle/HF mirror; the official Open-i site was under maintenance when checked) ([arXiv 2403.02469](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02469); [HF ykumards/open-i](https://huggingface.co/datasets/ykumards/open-i/commit/790f315)); ROCOv2 adds 79,789 captioned multi-modality figures under CC BY-NC ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/record/10821435)). If DICOM is a requirement (for a HealthImaging demo), RSNA Pneumonia 2018 is real DICOM behind a free Kaggle account ([RSNA](https://www.rsna.org/education/ai-resources-and-training/ai-image-challenge/rsna-pneumonia-detection-challenge-2018)) and NCI IDC is CC-BY oncology DICOM on the AWS Open Data Registry that HealthImaging can import directly ([AWS registry IDC](https://registry.opendata.aws/nci-imaging-data-commons); [HealthImaging cross-account import](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2024/05/aws-healthimaging-cross-account-data-imports)).

The deeper structural fact is that **no open dataset links real labs, imaging and ECG for the same patient**; that linkage exists only in the credentialed MIMIC-IV family. The one open substitute is the **Synthea Coherent Data Set**: 1,278 synthetic FHIR R4 bundles with linked DICOM (299 objects), ECG, DNA and clinical notes, CC BY 4.0, on public S3 at `s3://synthea-open-data/coherent/` ([AWS registry Coherent](https://registry.opendata.aws/synthea-coherent-data/); [Electronics 2022](https://www.doi.org/10.3390/electronics11081199)). The pragmatic design that follows is to use one Coherent patient (or a MultiCaRe case) as the end-to-end scenario the demo walks through, and to show each specialist persona on real data from PTB-XL, MIMIC-IV demo and Open-i so the judges see both a coherent story and genuine inputs.

## The linked repos are architecture references, not one-day starting points

The six "sample repositories" split into three aws-samples repos, two academic repos and one Kaggle dataset misfiled as a repo. **sample-healthcare-agents** is a Strands-on-AgentCore fullstack for prior authorization and revenue cycle (eligibility, medical coding via Comprehend Medical, EDI 837P claims, appeals), with HealthLake FHIR R4 via AgentCore Gateway, Cognito, an Amplify/Next.js frontend and a CDK deploy; it expects Claude Sonnet access, Node 20+, Python 3.10+ and the CDK CLI, and the README notes "nothing is written automatically" ([sample-healthcare-agents](https://github.com/aws-samples/sample-healthcare-agents)). **sample-agentic-radmapper** is the only radiology item: an orchestrator on AgentCore Runtime that routes exams to radiologists via an Exam Metadata Synthesizer, a Patient History Synthesizer and a Rad Assignment agent with availability and SLA sub-agents, using Bedrock Guardrails as a PHI gatekeeper and flagging urgent findings such as intracranial hemorrhage; it is "for demonstration purposes only" and its prerequisites live in a notebook ([sample-agentic-radmapper](https://github.com/aws-samples/sample-agentic-radmapper)). Its value to Track 1 is the orchestrator-plus-synthesizer shape and the Guardrails wiring, not the scheduling logic. **amazon-bedrock-agents-healthcare-lifesciences** is a catalog of 36+ Strands agents (drug research, clinical trials, genomics, terminology) with MCP servers, skills, a Kiro Power and an `agentcore_template/`; its shared `Infra_cfn.yaml` provisions a VPC, Redshift cluster, S3, a Bedrock Knowledge Base and an execution role, which is far too heavy for one day, and the README names no lab, radiology, EKG or nutrition agent ([HCLS toolkit](https://github.com/aws-samples/amazon-bedrock-agents-healthcare-lifesciences)). Use `agentcore_template/` and individual catalog entries, skip the CloudFormation.

The two academic repos are worth reading for patterns and nothing else. **MedAgent-Pro** (ICLR 2026) is a hierarchical planner/examiner/evaluator/RAG workflow for evidence-based multimodal diagnosis with segmentation and grounding tools, but it requires an OpenAI API key by default and its only worked example is glaucoma ([MedAgent-Pro](https://github.com/jinlab-imvr/MedAgent-Pro)). **MIMIC-Ext-CXR-QBA** is a ~42M-pair chest X-ray VQA benchmark whose data sits on PhysioNet behind credentialing ([mimic-ext-cxr-qba](https://github.com/philip-mueller/mimic-ext-cxr-qba)). The sixth item, "Clinical Notes and Diagnosis Code," is a Kaggle dataset and notebook for clinical-note-to-ICD workflows.

Beyond the page, four AWS samples are closer to what a Track 1 team needs. The AgentCore samples' **healthcare-appointment-agent** already wires Strands or LangGraph to HealthLake through Gateway with Cognito OAuth and tools like `get_patient_emr` and `search_immunization_emr` ([AWS ML Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/building-health-care-agents-using-amazon-bedrock-agentcore)). The Strands samples' **medical-document-processing-assistant** is pure Python with no infrastructure and extracts diagnoses, medications and treatments with ICD-10, RxNorm and SNOMED codes ([strands-agents/samples](https://github.com/strands-agents/samples/tree/main/python/04-industry-use-cases)). **sample-cancer-treatment-planning-module** is the cleanest template for the orchestrator-plus-specialists shape: a Treatment Planning Orchestrator calling a Patient Multimodal Data Synthesizer (HealthLake + HealthImaging), a Clinical History Extractor and an Evidence-based Guidelines agent that uses Strands' native `retrieve` tool over NCCN/ASCO guidelines in a Bedrock Knowledge Base, with an oncologist-in-the-loop approval step ([AWS for Industries](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/industries/accelerate-breast-cancer-treatment-planning-with-agentic-ai)). **sample-multiagent-multimodal-hcls** (August 2026) is the most complete multimodal reference, a Strands supervisor calling clinical, imaging, genomic, trials and PubMed sub-agents over A2A, each its own AgentCore Runtime, with HealthLake and HealthImaging reached through their official MCP servers; it deploys six runtimes plus Amplify, so read it for the architecture diagram rather than cloning it ([AWS for Industries part 2](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/industries/multi-agent-multimodal-data-analysis-on-aws-part-2-multi-agent-orchestration-and-predictive-analytics/)). The official **awslabs/mcp** repo supplies HealthLake and HealthImaging MCP servers (tools such as `search_resources`, `patient_everything` and DICOM datastore operations) that plug into Strands or Gateway without custom code ([awslabs/mcp](https://github.com/awslabs/mcp); [HealthLake MCP blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/industries/building-healthcare-ai-agents-with-open-source-aws-healthlake-mcp-server/)). The `awslabs/amazon-bedrock-agent-samples` library linked from Track 3 is the general-purpose Bedrock multi-agent collection and is just as usable here ([Track 3](https://atx.rahman.dev/track-3.html)).

## Pick Strands agents-as-tools on AgentCore Runtime and ignore the rest of the stack

The AWS agentic stack has grown to the point where choosing is the main risk. AgentCore went GA on October 13, 2025 and now spans Runtime, Memory, Gateway, Identity, Code Interpreter, Browser, Observability, Policy, Evaluations, Optimization, Registry, Payments and a managed Harness ([AgentCore GA](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/10/amazon-bedrock-agentcore-available); [AgentCore docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock-agentcore/latest/devguide/what-is-bedrock-agentcore.html)). Bedrock Agents is now "Amazon Bedrock Agents Classic," in maintenance mode and **closed to new customers since July 30, 2026**, which means a Workshop Studio account created today cannot create one; AWS's own recommendation is AgentCore ([Agents Classic maintenance mode](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/agents-classic-maintenance-mode.html)). Bedrock Flows remains a low-code deterministic pipeline builder, not an agent framework. The decision table for a one-day build:

| Layer | Pick | Skip | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Orchestration | Strands Agents SDK, agents-as-tools pattern | Swarm, Graph, A2A, LangGraph rewrites | Agents-as-tools is literally `Agent(tools=[specialist_agent])`; one orchestrator plus 3-5 specialists deploys as a single runtime and stays deterministic in a demo ([Strands docs](https://strandsagents.com/latest/documentation/docs/user-guide/concepts/multi-agent/agents-as-tools/)) |
| Hosting | AgentCore Runtime via the kit's `agentcore` CLI; a local Streamlit UI if deploy stalls | Manual ECR/ARM64 container path | The kit already proves the Runtime path in these accounts; the manual path needs ARM64 Docker builds |
| AgentCore extras | Gateway only if you want MCP tools; Memory only if you demo multi-turn | Policy, Evaluations, Optimization, Registry, Payments, Browser | Setup time without demo value |
| Model | Whatever `list-inference-profiles` shows; GPT-5.6 Luna is the tested default; Claude Sonnet 4.5 / Haiku 4.5 if allowlisted | Hardcoded model IDs from samples | Samples from late 2025 assume Claude 4.x; swap IDs to match the allowlist ([Bedrock Anthropic model cards](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-cards-anthropic.html)) |
| Clinical NLP | Comprehend Medical (`detect_entities_v2`, `infer_icd10_cm`, `infer_rx_norm`) wrapped as `@tool` | HealthScribe, Connect Health | No provisioning; per-unit pricing with a free tier ([AWS compare](https://aws.amazon.com/compare/comprehend-medical-and-healthscribe/)) |
| FHIR / DICOM | HealthLake with Synthea preload (create it first thing) | HealthImaging unless mocked | Datastore creation and import take tens of minutes; DICOM import jobs are a stretch |
| Safety | Bedrock Guardrails with PII ANONYMIZE plus custom regex for MRNs | Relying on Guardrails for tool outputs | Guardrails inspects text in/out only, not `toolUse.input` or `toolResult` ([Guardrails sensitive filters](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-sensitive-filters.html)) |
| Grounding | Bedrock Knowledge Base over a few guideline PDFs plus Strands' `retrieve` tool | Bedrock Data Automation pipelines | A KB over a handful of PDFs is a 20-40 minute console job; BDA is asynchronous and adds latency |
| IDE | Browser VS Code; Kiro for one person's scaffolding | Whole team on Kiro | Kiro's free tier is reported at 50 credits/month ([Kiro billing](https://kiro.dev/docs/billing/); [third-party pricing](https://usagebar.com/blog/kiro-pricing-and-free-tier)) |

Strands 1.0 ships four multi-agent primitives (agents-as-tools, Swarm, Graph, Workflow) plus A2A ([AWS Open Source Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/opensource/introducing-strands-agents-1-0-production-ready-multi-agent-orchestration-made-simple/)). The agents-as-tools page's minimal example is the entire scaffold you need: a specialist `Agent(system_prompt=..., callback_handler=None)` passed in the orchestrator's `tools=[...]`, with `.as_tool()` to customize name and description and `preserve_context=True` to keep sub-agent history. Deploying it means importing `BedrockAgentCoreApp`, decorating the handler with `@app.entrypoint`, and calling `app.run()`; async generator handlers support streaming, which is how you make handoffs visible in real time ([Strands docs: Deploy to AgentCore](https://strandsagents.com/latest/documentation/docs/user-guide/deploy/deploy_to_bedrock_agentcore/python/)). The closest ready-made curriculum is the "Getting Started with Strands Agents" workshop, whose multi-agent topology module and "Lab 10c - Deploy to AgentCore" cover exactly this in a morning ([Strands workshop](https://catalog.workshops.aws/strands-agents/en-US/20-multi-agent-topology)).

Kiro is optional but can be a force multiplier for one person: its Specs (requirements, design, tasks), Hooks and Powers scaffold Strands/AgentCore boilerplate, the HCLS toolkit ships a `powers/hcls-agentcore-builder/` Power, and a Kiro spec doubles as the design document you show judges ([Kiro docs](https://kiro.dev/docs/); [HCLS toolkit](https://github.com/aws-samples/amazon-bedrock-agents-healthcare-lifesciences)). On the healthcare service side, HealthLake and HealthImaging both have official MCP servers, and the HealthLake server's `patient_everything` tool returns a full patient bundle in one call, which is exactly the "Patient Multimodal Data Synthesizer" step in the cancer sample ([HealthLake MCP blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/industries/building-healthcare-ai-agents-with-open-source-aws-healthlake-mcp-server/)). The one architectural trap is Guardrails: its sensitive-information filter can BLOCK or ANONYMIZE 30+ PII types including NAME, AGE, ADDRESS, US_SSN and CA_HEALTH_NUMBER, but it "evaluates text content only" and does not touch tool arguments or results, so a FHIR bundle flowing through a tool call is unmasked unless you run Comprehend Medical `detect_phi` inside the tool ([Guardrails sensitive filters](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-sensitive-filters.html)). With synthetic data this is a demo talking point rather than a risk, and saying it out loud is a credibility signal.

For the Radiologist persona specifically, do not let a general Bedrock model narrate pixels. A 2025 RSNA Radiology case review of four open chest X-ray models found each with distinct errors: CheXagent produced a false-positive apical pneumothorax, MAIRA-2 missed a malpositioned feeding tube, and MedGemma hallucinated a chest tube ([RSNA](https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/abs/10.1148/radiol.253456)). The safer and faster scope is to have the Radiologist persona interpret an existing report (Open-i gives you real ones) and, if you want image input, call MedGemma 4B as a tool whose output is labeled as a model finding with confidence; MedGemma's own card says it is "not intended to directly inform diagnosis" ([HF google/medgemma-4b-it](https://huggingface.co/google/medgemma-4b-it)). The same logic applies to ECG: ECG-R1's authors report that "severe hallucinations are widespread in existing ECG models" ([arXiv 2602.04279](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.04279)), so the pattern that works is a classifier or foundation model (ECG-FM is MIT-licensed with Hugging Face checkpoints, fine-tuned AUROC 0.996 for AF ([GitHub bowang-lab/ECG-FM](https://github.com/bowang-lab/ECG-FM/.))) or PTB-XL's own cardiologist report as the tool output, and the Doctor persona reasoning over structured findings plus confidence rather than the trace.

## Judges reward a visible safety architecture, not accuracy claims

The comparable-project evidence converges on a short list of differentiators. In peer-reviewed work, **MDAgents** (NeurIPS 2024 Oral) found that adding a moderator review plus retrieved external knowledge raised average accuracy by **11.8%**, more than any gain from the collaboration structure itself; adding structure beat adding agents ([NeurIPS 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/90d1fc07f46e31387978b88e7e057a31-Abstract.html)). **MedAgentBench**, a FHIR-based virtual EHR with 300 physician-written tasks, found the best model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2) at 69.67% overall but **100% on one-step tasks versus 23.33% on three-or-more-step tasks** ([arXiv 2501.14654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14654); [ibl.ai table](https://ibl.ai/blog/hospital-ai-agents-single-turn-tests-grade-the-actions)); keep each persona's tool chain to one or two steps and show the trace. **AgentClinic** showed that converting static MedQA into sequential interactive diagnosis can drop accuracy below one-tenth of the static score, and that a persistent "notebook" tool gave Llama-3 up to a 92% relative gain, which argues for a shared case state that every persona reads and writes ([arXiv 2405.07960](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07960v4)). The newest systems add exactly the structure the track's guardrails ask for: **TumorBoard** (August 2026) pairs five specialist agents with an adversarial critic and a safety governor over a claim-evidence ledger, and under evidence deletion deferred **84.2%** of unsafe cases while limiting harmful recommendations to 5.8% ([arXiv 2608.03190](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03190)); **Cardiologent** (July 2026) runs one agent per signal, integrates them into a patient-level rhythm profile, and has a critic check each conclusion against the cited guideline ([arXiv 2607.25340](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25340)); Hippocratic AI's **Polaris** uses deterministic orchestration code around a primary agent plus specialist support agents for Medication, Labs & Vitals, Nutrition and a Human Intervention agent whose task "wins conflicts," evaluated by more than 1,100 nurses and 130 physicians ([arXiv 2403.13313](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.13313v1)).

The hackathon-scale evidence points the same way. **AI Tumor Board** (lablab.ai, June 2026) gives every specialist a structured output with a mandatory "dissent" field, stamps every recommendation `requires_human_review=true` with `human_signoff=null`, and runs its Safety Officer on a different model and framework from the specialists it reviews ([lablab submission](https://lablab.ai/submissions/mu5y0pjgonguw2al9bqbufop)). **Discharge Buddy** (lablab.ai, August 2026, shown with a first-place badge) extracts medications, appointments, warning signs and restrictions from a discharge summary into a human-editable form, serves separate caregiver and patient views, and grades voice check-ins against the patient's own plan into On Track / Mild Concern / Needs Attention with a "Call 911" panel for urgent cases ([lablab submission](https://lablab.ai/submissions/sx1vq47f4kg2cor08cb4id5r)). Neither reports accuracy metrics; both win on structure a judge can see on screen. Published judging language from other clinical hackathons (BeHealth 2026: patient safety, clinical workflow fit, validation evidence, usability, responsible implementation; Oxford: clinical usefulness, safety, scalability) reads like the Track 1 guardrails list ([BeHealth 2026](https://www.b2match.com/e/behealth-2026/components/69652/fjaQILHOOTvF); [Oxford clinical hackathon](https://www.chch.ox.ac.uk/events/clinical-hackathon)).

Two failure modes are documented well enough to design against, and both can be turned into demo moments. A Mount Sinai study in Communications Medicine (August 2025) planted one fabricated detail in each of 300 physician-validated vignettes and found six LLMs propagated it **50-82%** of the time; a one-line mitigation prompt cut the average from roughly 66% to 44%, and temperature 0 did not help ([PubMed 40753316](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40753316/)). Running that test on ten to twenty synthetic cases and showing the critic persona catching the planted lab value is a two-hour eval that references a peer-reviewed failure. Clinicians reviewing LLM-recommended regimens found a median of 4.1 to 6.9 medication errors per regimen ([Frontiers in Pharmacology](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1514445/pdf)), which is why "prescriptions" in the track brief should be framed as a Doctor-persona draft awaiting confirmation, never as output. For labs, the Lab-AI study showed that retrieval over MedlinePlus "Normal Results" lifted normal-range accuracy from 0.429 to **0.995** ([PMC12707499](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12707499)), and LOINC itself carries no reference ranges ([LOINC forum](https://forum.loinc.org/t/standardized-reference-ranges-for-loinc-codes/2571)), so the Lab Analyst persona must bring its own range table (MIMIC-IV demo's `d_labitems` or a MedlinePlus-derived JSON) and display the retrieved range next to every interpretation. The RubRIX rubric (Findings of ACL 2026) offers five clinician-validated caregiver-risk dimensions (Inattention, Bias & Stigma, Information Inaccuracy, Uncritical Affirmation, Epistemic Arrogance) with released datasets; rubric-guided refinement cut risk components 45-98% in one iteration, making it a ready-made scoring frame for the patient-facing output ([arXiv 2601.13235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13235)).

The caregiver framing is itself under-served. Academic multi-agent work targets clinicians; caregiver-facing AI is pilots and vendor pages, with 33% of caregivers using caregiver-specific tech per AARP 2025 ([AARP report](https://www.ageinplacetech.com/files/aip/2025-technology-trends-older-adults-caregiving.doi_.10.26419-2fres.00891.007.pdf)) and caregivers in the Carey study expressing hesitation about persistent storage of family data ([arXiv 2506.15047](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15047v1)). A GPT-4 discharge-summary translation study in NEJM AI improved subjective comprehension by 2.4 points with the largest gains among lower-health-literacy readers ([2 Minute Medicine summary](https://www.2minutemedicine.com/large-language-models-llms-substantially-improved-patient-comprehension-of-discharge-summaries/)), which is the evidence base for the "patient-friendly result" the brief ends on. The pitfalls to avoid are the inverse of the winners: free-form diagnosis, no reference-range or guideline retrieval, no uncertainty field, no escalation path, realistic-looking PHI, long tool chains, an LLM narrating raw pixels or waveforms, and no eval slide.

## Conclusion

The track's stated requirements are loose, but the environment and the evidence together narrow the winning design to something quite specific: a Strands orchestrator with three to five specialist personas, one of which is a critic that is not on the organizer's list, running on the kit's AgentCore Runtime with whichever model the allowlist exposes, over one scripted synthetic patient whose labs, ECG report and radiology report come from open sources, with a shared case ledger that records who reviewed what, a reference-range or guideline snippet beside every claim, a red-flag interrupt that waits for a human click, and a patient-facing summary scored against a visible rubric. The organizers' own guardrails list is the rubric, the AWS judges will notice sponsor-stack fluency and IAM/cleanup hygiene, and the literature says the critic plus retrieval is worth more than a sixth persona.

Three decisions should be made in the first hour because everything downstream depends on them: run `aws bedrock list-inference-profiles` and pin the model; choose the demo patient source (Synthea Coherent for a linked record, MultiCaRe for a narrative case) and download PTB-XL, the MIMIC-IV demo and a slice of Open-i before anything else; and decide whether HealthLake is in scope, since its datastore must be created early or not at all. The idea-selection space that remains open and matches both the brief and the caregiver angle is transitions of care: a results-explainer for a family caregiver, a medication reconciliation pass with pharmacist-persona explanations, or a discharge-plan-to-care-tasks flow, each of which lets the Lab Analyst, Radiologist and Doctor hand off visibly to Nutritionist and Physio personas and end on a plain-language answer a patient could actually use.
